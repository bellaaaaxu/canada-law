## What the law says

The BC *Employment Standards Act*, s. 63(1)–(2), states:

> **63 (1)** After 3 consecutive months of employment, the employer becomes liable to pay an employee an amount equal to one week's wages as compensation for length of service.
>
> **(2)** The employer's liability for compensation for length of service increases as follows:
> (a) after 12 consecutive months of employment, to an amount equal to **2 weeks' wages**;
> (b) after 3 consecutive years of employment, to an amount equal to 3 weeks' wages plus one additional week's wages for each additional year of employment, to a maximum of 8 weeks' wages.

The employer can discharge this liability by giving **written notice** of the same length instead, or a combination of notice and pay (s. 63(3)).

Also relevant — s. 63(5):

> "the employment of an employee who is laid off for more than a temporary layoff is deemed to have been terminated at the beginning of the layoff."

And the definition in s. 1:

> "temporary layoff" means … (b) in any other case, a layoff of up to 13 weeks in any period of 20 consecutive weeks.

## What it means

Under BC law, if you are laid off (and it is not a "temporary layoff" of 13 weeks or less), it counts as a termination of employment. When that happens, your employer owes you **compensation for length of service** — what people commonly call "severance." With 2 years of consecutive employment, the statutory amount is **2 weeks' wages**. The employer can satisfy this by paying you, giving you 2 weeks' written notice before your last day, or a combination of the two.

## What decides the outcome

The following facts determine how the rule applies to a specific situation:

- **Length of consecutive employment** — how many months/years you worked continuously.
- **Whether written notice was given** — if the employer gave proper written notice, they may not owe additional pay.
- **Whether the layoff is "temporary"** — a layoff of 13 weeks or less in a 20-week period is a temporary layoff, not a termination; compensation for length of service would not yet be triggered.
- **Whether you have a right of recall** — this changes the definition of "temporary layoff."
- **Whether the termination was for just cause** — just cause removes the employer's liability (s. 63(3)(c)).
- **Whether you are in a federally regulated industry** — most restaurants are provincially regulated, but it's worth confirming. Federal workplaces (banks, airlines, telecom, etc.) fall under different rules this tool does not cover.
